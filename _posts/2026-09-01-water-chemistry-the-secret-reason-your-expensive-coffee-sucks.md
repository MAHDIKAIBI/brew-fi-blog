They adjust water temperature or alter their pouring speed. These adjustments fail because the problem is architectural. Coffee grounds make up less than two percent of your mug. Water does all the heavy chemical work.</p><p>To fix this issue, people buy total dissolved solids meters. They dip the probe into tap water and see a reading. A reading of one hundred and fifty parts per million feels reassuring. However, that single number is a total illusion.</p><p>A meter measures total dissolved mass in liquid. It cannot tell you what those minerals actually are. Two different water sources can show the exact same mineral reading. Instead, the cup tastes lifeless and flat. You might blame your water supply for being too acidic. But a test strip shows a neutral p H level.</p><p>Measuring p H gives coffee lovers a false sense of security. The instantaneous p H level only shows hydrogen ions present right now. It does not show how water behaves when coffee acids enter. Coffee researcher Doctor Marco Wellinger studied this exact process.</p><p>He found that alkalinity impacts perceived acidity far more than starting p H level. Alkalinity measures the buffering capacity of your water supply. It determines how strongly water resists changes in acidity.</p><p>Birgit Kohler from <span data-semantic-id="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brita">Brita</span> warns that high bicarbonate ruins delicate acids. Pure water seems like a clean canvas for coffee flavor. But zero mineral water produces a thin and hollow brew.</p><p>In twenty fourteen, chemist Christopher Hendon studied water at the <span data-semantic-id="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bath">University of Bath</span>. His team showed that water acts as an active chemical magnet. Positively charged cations like magnesium and calcium pull flavors from grounds.</p><p>Using quantum modeling, researchers mapped how minerals grab target molecules. Magnesium and calcium act like microscopic hooks. They pull organic acids and caffeine into the liquid. Without these minerals, delicate flavor notes stay trapped in grounds.</p><p>Magnesium ions extract bright fruit notes with intense energy. Calcium ions bind strongly but create a heavier body. Coffee enthusiasts often load water with extra magnesium.</p><p>However, higher chemical extraction does not guarantee better flavor. An espresso shot uses only one point five grams of water. Espresso uses ten times less water per gram of coffee.
